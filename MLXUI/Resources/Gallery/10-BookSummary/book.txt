Chapter One: The Habit Loop

Every habit starts the same way: a cue triggers a routine, and
the routine delivers a reward. The cue can be a time of day, a
location, an emotional state, or the presence of certain people.
Once the loop runs enough times, the brain stops evaluating it
and starts craving it automatically. This is why habits feel
involuntary even when we consciously dislike them.

Chapter Two: Changing the Routine

You rarely delete a habit outright; you replace its routine while
keeping the cue and reward intact. If stress at 3pm always sends
you to the vending machine, the fix isn't willpower at 3pm, it's
finding a different routine, a walk, a call to a friend, that
satisfies the same underlying craving for relief.

Chapter Three: Keystone Habits

Some habits reorganize everything around them. Regular exercise,
for instance, tends to improve sleep, diet, and mood without
those being deliberately targeted. Identifying and installing one
keystone habit is often more effective than trying to overhaul a
dozen small behaviors at once.

Chapter Four: Habits at Scale

Organizations have habits too, embedded in how meetings run, how
decisions get escalated, and how mistakes get reported. Changing
one visible organizational habit, like how safety incidents are
disclosed, can shift the culture around it far more than a new
policy document ever could.
